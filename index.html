I am interested in how we design and manage the systems that feed us. Through my work with farmers and food aid, I have witnessed how often federal and state programs fall short due to gaps between policy design and lived experience. To address this gap, I am seeking opportunities to amend the relationship between food, city, and architecture; specifically, how the physical design of our cities shapes what people eat, how they access food, and whether they can grow it themselves.

My economics thesis examined how farmers turn waste into value, giving me a framework for thinking about municipal waste systems, byproduct management, and regulatory incentives. My architecture thesis investigated how community gardens can serve as crisis-response infrastructure, exploring how physical design shapes social outcomes. This has given me a systems-level perspective that I want to apply to creating inclusive market design that helps small-scale producers access institutional and retail channels.

Project 01 · Economics honors thesis

Closing the Poop Loop: The Creation of a Manure Market in India's Gobar Dhan Scheme

Amherst College · Advised by Prof. Jessica Reyes · 2026

Manure is a valuable fertilizer input that rarely reaches the fields that need it, because it's too costly to transport far from the livestock that produce it. India's 2018 Gobar Dhan scheme tries to fix this by installing village-scale biogas plants that buy raw dung and distribute nutrient-rich slurry back to local farmers. I built a village-level panel from plant registry and water-quality records to test whether this actually shifts farmers away from chemical fertilizer, using water turbidity as a proxy for nutrient runoff.

Key finding: No detectable average effect on turbidity. But villages farther from fertilizer shops see meaningfully larger reductions, consistent with the theory that substitution incentives bind only where chemical fertilizer is genuinely costly to reach.

Project 02 · Architectural Studies honors thesis

From Plan to Plot: Designing for Long-Term Crisis Response in Community Gardens

Amherst College · Advised by Prof. Gabriel Arboleda · 2026

When federal food assistance is threatened, why do some community gardens become vital sites of crisis response while others fall into neglect? I ran a diagnostic, mixed-methods study of seven Nuestras Raíces gardens in Holyoke, MA — combining municipal planning documents, semi-structured interviews, and GIS analysis of land cover, walkability, and social infrastructure — structured around Importance-Performance Analysis.

Key finding: "Importance" is institutionally fragmented between city-led revitalization logic and housing-authority amenity logic. Gardens thrive where community-driven political action secures space for cultural practice rather than where land tenure is most secure.

Project 03 · Geospatial Economics Capstone

e-NAM’s Cross-Market Price Impact: Evidence from India’s Wheat Mandis

I evaluate the causal impact of enrollment in India's electronic trading platform, e-NAM (National Agriculture Market), on wheat price convergence across agricultural markets (mandis) between 2003 and 2019. Utilizing an event study design, the study measures price dispersion across two standardized metrics—within-APMC prices benchmarked against the APMC average and across-APMC prices benchmarked against the state average—while controlling for local credit levels, standard of living, mandi wheat suitability, and geographic border proximity.

Key finding: Contrary to policy expectations, e-NAM enrollment did not facilitate market integration or lead to price convergence across regional markets. Suggests that simply digitizing bidding is insufficient to overcome underlying market imperfections—such as physical transport costs, quality verification issues, and entrenched local trader networks.

Project 04 · Field research, School for Field Studies

Assessing Ecosystem Services in the Chianti Classico Region

with Bella Woodbury & Maizie Schaffner · Tuscany, Italy

Working with two collaborators, I helped design a replicable methodology for assessing ecosystem service delivery across nine vineyard sites in Chianti Classico — triangulating farmer survey responses, direct field observation, and open-source geospatial data (land use, elevation, rainfall erosivity) to evaluate landscape maintenance, soil erosion control, and water runoff control.

Key finding: Two farms at similar elevation sit at opposite ends of the rainfall-erosivity range — a gap only explained by pairing the GIS layers with what farmers told us about soil composition, terracing, and drainage on their own land.

Fieldwork

Twelve farms, one field season.

Underlying the Chianti Classico research were farm visits across Tuscany and the Aeolian Islands with the School for Field Studies. Almost every grower, regardless of what they farm, described the same three recurring problems — the lens I used to take these notes.

01

Regional Farm of Maremma

Public farm · 4,000 ha · Maremmana cattle & horses +
  • Public farm funded by taxes and cooperative investment — the largest visited, at 4,000 ha (720 arable, 650 natural pasture, 1,800 forest, 650 pine).
  • Runs reproductive and meat lines of Maremmana cattle, a breed said to descend from an ancient wild bovine; its Maremmano horses trace to the Etruscan period and are ridden by mounted herders (butteri) to manage the herd.
  • Butteri track lineage and genetic diversity by DNA-tagging every animal and culling aggressive bulls from breeding; pregnant cows are isolated in a "nursery" to protect calves from wolves.
  • Rising soil salinity from sea-level rise and historic land drainage is reducing plant diversity — the cattle graze salt-tolerant plants like Salicornia as an adaptation.
  • Entirely self-sufficient on forage grown within a pasture-crop rotation; water comes from wells, a windpump, solar-powered pumps, and creek flow.
  • A rare case of government intervention succeeding at breed conservation — the farm's model has since been referenced by organizations including the FAO.
02

Fattoria Latte Maremma

Family dairy · 28 cows · 39 ha +
  • 28 cows on 39 ha; manure is returned directly to the land in a closed-loop fertility system.
  • Organic carrying capacity is capped at 2 cows/ha, versus 1.4/ha under conventional EU (CAP) rules.
  • Water use is under half of industrial norms — 25 L/cow/day here versus roughly 60 L on industrial dairy farms.
  • Certified-organic rules forbid the antibiotics that high-density conventional dairies rely on, since residues can pass into the milk.
  • No refrigerated transport on-site; milk is pasteurized off-site at a regional Latte Maremma facility.
  • About 30% of income now comes from agritourism — the farm's tightest constraints are social and logistical (year-round commitment, no room for employees) more than financial.
03

Terra di Sacra

1,050 ha · Italy's first WWF Oasi · Maremmana cattle +
  • 1,050 ha total (600 cultivated), including Italy's first WWF-protected "Oasi" — a former wetland now recognized by the EU as a "Life Project."
  • 60 Maremmana cattle on a reproductive line sold to retailers in Rome and Verona; breeding stays entirely natural (no artificial insemination) to protect the at-risk breed's genetics.
  • Seawater intrusion is salinizing groundwater and lowering fertility, forcing a shift to winter-only crops on a 3-year rotation (wheat, medicinal herbs, and others).
  • The farm deliberately avoids cross-breeding its cattle, relying instead on regional conservation payments for at-risk breeds.
  • Agritourism (in former sharecropper housing) coexists with the cattle without competing for land — the animals are kept partly for what they contribute to the landscape itself.
04

San Carlo Rice Paddies

480 ha, Parco della Maremma · Organic rice since the 1960s +
  • 480 ha of former marsh and pine forest inside the regional park; grows rice — unusual for Tuscany — because the clay-heavy soil holds water exceptionally well.
  • Organic transition follows 3 rules: no chemicals, rye-grass cover crops, and crop rotation (wheat, oat, chickpea, sunflower) — targeting the same three problems every farm on this page names: fertility, weeds, pests.
  • Nitrogen-fixing green manure replaces synthetic fertilizer; rice is seeded rather than transplanted to cut water use, with fields flooded twice via an underground public pipeline (roughly €30,000/year in water fees).
  • Grows two Italian rice varieties — a starchier Carnaroli favored in winter, a lighter variety favored in summer — and reuses the milling husk as building material or fertilizer.
  • 70% sold wholesale to a Florence supermarket chain; the remainder sold locally to a single loyal customer.
05

Le Fonti & the Panzano Transition

Village-wide shift to organic, triggered by a 2007 pest outbreak +
  • A 2007 outbreak of flavescenza dorata (an insect-borne vine disease) triggered a government mandate to spray pesticides village-wide, regardless of whether a given farm was actually infected.
  • Panzano's growers negotiated an alternative — academic monitoring instead of blanket spraying — which ended up accelerating the village's shift to organic viticulture.
  • Spevis, a viticulture research station, works farm by farm rather than at scale, building a location-specific knowledge base and promoting low-input remedies like homeopathic sprays and distilled wood water.
  • A 2023 outbreak of peronospora (downy mildew) exposed the limits of rigid calendar-based spraying and the cost of poor information-sharing — and pushed growers toward shared distributors and price transparency.
06

Castello di Verrazzano

50 ha · High-end wine · Active soil regeneration +
  • A high-value model — roughly 1 kg of fruit becomes a €60 bottle of wine — funds labor-intensive, idealistic practices most farms can't afford.
  • Underlying geology (schist and limestone) shifts sharply across a 250–500 m elevation change, making farm-wide soil management genuinely difficult.
  • Years of tilling caused erosion and falling yields; the farm has stopped tilling in most zones and is regenerating soil selectively — none at the hilltop, where thin, rocky soil already competes hard with young vines for water.
  • Uses zeolite rock dust to protect leaves from sun stress and wood vinegar to cut sulfur use via induced systemic resistance; copper remains the only effective treatment for one persistent fungus.
  • Weeds are controlled mechanically — a toothed wheel mounds soil along the vine row to smother them without hydraulic equipment.
  • Currently at 50% soil cover, working toward 100% for full regeneration; also scoping a "Vitiforest" agroforestry project — nitrogen-fixing, high-trunked trees to buffer wind without over-shading the vines.
07

Fontodi

100 ha · Chianina cattle · monoculture cover crop +
  • Chianina cattle — the breed behind Florentine steak — were nearly lost to mechanization in the 1750s and were saved largely by continued market demand for the meat.
  • Regular breeds receive a flat EU/CAP payment; breeds classified "at risk of extinction" receive double.
  • Cattle here are raised mainly for manure, composted to enrich vineyard soil, rather than primarily for meat.
  • Copper and orange-oil extract treat a leaf fungus by drying the leaf surface; the cover crop between vine rows is currently a monoculture rather than a diverse mix.
  • Farm philosophy, in the grower's words: "to make great wine you need great soil."
08

Mugello Chestnut Plantation

800 m elevation · PGI-protected · ~150-year-old trees +
  • A historically poor region — slopes too steep for row-crop farming pushed chestnuts to become the local staple generations ago.
  • Nuts are hand-harvested (machinery scars them, making them unsellable) from trees roughly 100–150 years old; unsellable nuts are dried over chestnut-wood fires and milled into flour.
  • Sales are protected by a PGI (Protected Geographical Indication) designation — it sets compulsory production standards, but also invites price-gouging middlemen once nuts reach larger markets.
  • Ground cover is deliberately kept as moss rather than grass; cattle graze to suppress the grass, since moss retains humidity and limits erosion and weeds.
  • An invasive gall wasp from Vietnam was controlled by introducing a natural predator; the farm is now investigating whether reintroducing native truffle fungi could suppress ink disease, a fungus that kills chestnut trees from the top down.
  • Warming temperatures are pushing the viable growing altitude higher — a short-term yield boost, but a longer-term threat as the climate keeps shifting.
09

Fattoria di Selvoli

30 ha (+40 ha exposed) · Ancient wheat, spelt, rye +
  • Family farm growing 4–5 ancient wheat varieties plus spelt and rye — all lower-gluten and in high demand — from seed saved on-site rather than purchased.
  • A 2–3 year rotation of nitrogen-fixing legumes and wheat rebuilds soil structure without any purchased fertilizer or animal manure.
  • Ancient grains resist most disease but remain vulnerable to a fungus that infects the grain's interior; copper is applied to seed only in wet conditions, at under 100 g/ha.
  • Mixing 15 wheat varieties in one field ("population" planting) spreads root depth and lets shorter plants physically brace taller ones against wind — an old-agriculture answer to lodging.
  • A shortened supply chain captures far more value: unprocessed wheat sells around €20/kg, but this farm's milled, packaged flour sells for up to €200/kg.
  • EU agri-environmental subsidies mostly favor large farms; this operation instead relies on WWOOF volunteer labor and membership in the Casentino biodistrict for informal, collective bargaining power.
10

Virgona Farm (Salina)

Family farm since the 1960s · 200+ tiny plots · 70 products +
  • Farms wine grapes and capers across more than 200 small allotments scattered over the island of Salina — a legacy of historically fragmented land division.
  • Produces roughly 70 different products (wine, capers, beer, jams) — an unusual degree of diversification for the island, where most farms specialize narrowly.
  • Beer is brewed on the mainland because imported water's mineral content is inconsistent with the island's own supply; the farm is exploring an on-site brewery using purified seawater.
  • Not certified organic — not for lack of practices, but because the sheer number of small, irregular plots makes certification impractical and would confuse consumers with mixed labeling on the same product.
  • Grapes are sun-dried outdoors for 2–3 weeks to concentrate sugars; all fermentation and bottling happens on-site, with products sold direct to consumers and restaurants as far away as Australia and California.
11

La Macina (Lipari)

33 ha · Ancient-grain mill · 80% of the island's fresh pasta +
  • Grows and mills ancient grain across 33 ha of mostly small, scattered plots (grain, olives, vineyard, fruit); the farm's restored mill supplies roughly 80% of the fresh pasta served in island restaurants.
  • Restoring and sharing that mill convinced other island farmers to start growing ancient grain themselves, since there was finally somewhere nearby to process it.
  • First produced beer in 2024, but — like Virgona Farm on Salina — currently brews off-island due to water-scarcity and mineral-consistency issues, with an on-site, purified-seawater brewery in the longer-term plan.
12

Podere Ruggeri

80 ha · Calvana cattle · Silvopastoral olive groves +
  • Silvopastoral system: Calvana cattle, sheep, and goats graze directly beneath the olive canopy, naturally fertilizing the groves and keeping grass short without machinery.
  • Calvana is a rare local Tuscan breed, well-adapted to poor forage and steep terrain, with an estimated 300 animals left; the farm works with a university program to prevent inbreeding among the last ~10 breeders.
  • Two Tuscan conservation laws underpin the breed's survival: direct payments for individually tagged, verified cows, and a grower-run brand with compulsory welfare rules (minimum 8 months/year of open grazing, specific feed requirements).
  • Feed is 80% farm-grown cereal and legume (maize is no longer viable in the heat); the rest is purchased hay, since dry pasture alone can't fatten cattle through increasingly hot, dry summers.
  • Diversifies into olive oil, three types of cheese, cured meats, and a two-room agritourism stay — largely because delayed wholesale payments make direct-to-consumer sales the most reliable source of immediate cash.
  • Manure from cows, sheep, and goats is collected and sold on to local wine producers — closing a fertility loop between livestock and viticulture across farms.
